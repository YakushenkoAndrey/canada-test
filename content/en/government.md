There are [[three key facts|number]] about Canada’s system of government: our country is a [[federal state, a parliamentary democracy and a constitutional monarchy|definition]].

@block government-02 section 28
@heading Federal state
There are [[federal, provincial, territorial and municipal governments|definition]] in Canada. The responsibilities of the federal and provincial governments were defined in 1867 in the British North America Act, now known as the [[Constitution Act, 1867|symbol]].

In our federal state, the [[federal government|definition]] takes responsibility for matters of national and international concern. These include [[defence, foreign policy, interprovincial trade and communications, currency, navigation, criminal law and citizenship|right-duty]]. The [[provinces|definition]] are responsible for [[municipal government, education, health, natural resources, property and civil rights, and highways|right-duty]]. The federal government and the provinces [[share jurisdiction over agriculture and immigration|right-duty]]. Federalism allows different provinces to adopt policies tailored to their own populations, and gives provinces the flexibility to experiment with new ideas and policies.

Every province has its own elected [[Legislative Assembly|definition]], like the House of Commons in Ottawa. The [[three northern territories|number]], which have small populations, do not have the status of provinces, but their governments and assemblies carry out many of the same functions.

@block government-03 section 28
@heading Parliamentary democracy
In Canada’s parliamentary democracy, the people elect members to the [[House of Commons in Ottawa|definition]] and to the provincial and territorial legislatures. These representatives are responsible for passing laws, approving and monitoring expenditures, and keeping the government accountable. Cabinet ministers are responsible to the elected representatives, which means they must retain the “[[confidence of the House|definition]]” and have to resign if they are defeated in a [[non-confidence vote|definition]].

[[Parliament has three parts: the Sovereign (Queen or King), the Senate and the House of Commons|definition]]. Provincial legislatures comprise the [[Lieutenant Governor and the elected Assembly|definition]].

In the federal government, the [[Prime Minister selects the Cabinet ministers|person]] and is responsible for the operations and policy of the government. The House of Commons is the representative chamber, made up of [[members of Parliament elected by the people|definition]], traditionally [[every four years|number]]. [[Senators are appointed by the Governor General on the advice of the Prime Minister and serve until age 75|definition]]. Both the House of Commons and the Senate consider and review [[bills (proposals for new laws)|definition]]. No bill can become law in Canada until it has been passed by both chambers and has received [[royal assent|definition]], granted by the Governor General on behalf of the Sovereign.

Living in a democracy, Canadian citizens have the right and the responsibility to participate in making decisions that affect them. It is important for [[Canadians aged 18 or more|number]] to participate in their democracy by [[voting in federal, provincial or territorial and municipal elections|right-duty]].

@block government-04 aside 29
@heading How a bill becomes law — The Legislative Process
Step 1. [[First Reading|definition]] – The bill is considered read for the first time and is printed.

Step 2. [[Second Reading|definition]] – Members debate the bill’s principle.

Step 3. [[Committee Stage|definition]] – Committee members study the bill clause by clause.

Step 4. [[Report Stage|definition]] – Members can make other amendments.

Step 5. [[Third Reading|definition]] – Members debate and vote on the bill.

Step 6. [[Senate|definition]] – The bill follows a similar process in the Senate.

Step 7. [[Royal Assent|definition]] – The bill receives royal assent after being passed by both Houses.

@block government-05 section 29
@heading Constitutional monarchy
@img p29-johnston.jpg David Johnston, 28th Governor General since Confederation, with grandchildren
As a constitutional monarchy, [[Canada’s Head of State is a hereditary Sovereign (Queen or King)|person]], who reigns in accordance with the Constitution: the rule of law. The Sovereign is a part of Parliament, playing an important, non-partisan role as the focus of citizenship and allegiance, most visibly during royal visits to Canada. Her Majesty is a [[symbol of Canadian sovereignty|symbol]], a guardian of constitutional freedoms, and a reflection of our history. The Royal Family’s example of lifelong service to the community is an encouragement for citizens to give their best to their country. As [[Head of the Commonwealth|person]], the Sovereign links Canada to [[53 other nations|number]] that cooperate to advance social, economic and cultural progress. Other constitutional monarchies include Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Australia, New Zealand, The Netherlands, Spain, Thailand, Japan, Jordan and Morocco.

There is a clear distinction in Canada between the [[head of state—the Sovereign|person]]—and the [[head of government—the Prime Minister|person]], who actually directs the governing of the country.

The Sovereign is represented in Canada by the [[Governor General|person]], who is appointed by the Sovereign on the advice of the Prime Minister, usually for [[five years|number]]. In each of the ten provinces, the Sovereign is represented by the [[Lieutenant Governor|person]], who is appointed by the Governor General on the advice of the Prime Minister, also normally for five years.

The interplay between the [[three branches of government—the Executive, Legislative and Judicial|definition]]—which work together but also sometimes in creative tension, helps to secure the rights and freedoms of Canadians.

Each provincial and territorial government has an elected legislature where provincial and territorial laws are passed. The members of the legislature are called [[members of the Legislative Assembly (MLAs), members of the National Assembly (MNAs), members of the Provincial Parliament (MPPs) or members of the House of Assembly (MHAs)|definition]], depending on the province or territory.

In each province, the [[Premier|person]] has a role similar to that of the Prime Minister in the federal government, just as the Lieutenant Governor has a role similar to that of the Governor General. In the three territories, the [[Commissioner|person]] represents the federal government and plays a ceremonial role.

@block government-06 aside 29
@heading Canada’s System of Government
[[Sovereign|person]] — represented in Canada by the Governor General.

[[Executive Branch|definition]] — Prime Minister and Cabinet.

[[Legislative Branch|definition]] — Parliament: the Sovereign; the Senate, appointed on the Prime Minister’s recommendation; and the House of Commons, elected by voters (government members and opposition members).

[[Judiciary|definition]] — the [[Supreme Court of Canada|symbol]] ([[nine judges appointed by the Governor General|number]]), the Federal Court of Canada and provincial courts.
